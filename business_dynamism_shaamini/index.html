Part I · The population
Companies House & the London Gazette

Company formation and dissolution over time.

Company formation has risen sharply over a generation: annual incorporations grew from approximately 63,000 in 2000 to some 470,000 by 2020, a sevenfold increase. Set against roughly 24,000 recorded insolvencies a year, the differential provides a measure of turnover within the corporate population.

Insolvencies denote formal insolvent procedures recorded in the London Gazette; solvent members' voluntary liquidations and voluntary strike-offs are excluded.

The London Gazette

The distribution of dissolution mechanisms.

Creditors' voluntary liquidation constitutes the most frequent route to dissolution. A substantial proportion of winding-up notices, approximately one quarter, correspond to solvent members' voluntary liquidation, whereby financially sound companies are wound up at the discretion of their owners.

Companies House & the London Gazette · geography

Where British business lives, and where it fails.

Company registrations concentrate heavily in the capital: London alone accounts for approximately 30 per cent of all UK companies, with the South East and North West next. Insolvencies broadly track that distribution.

Region from registered-office postcode. Scotland and Northern Ireland insolvencies are under-counted (they use the Edinburgh and Belfast Gazettes); about a fifth of notices lack a mappable postcode.

Part II · Dynamics of failure
The London Gazette

Insolvency and the economic cycle.

Recorded insolvencies rose markedly during the 2008 financial crisis before subsiding through the subsequent decade. The pandemic year of 2020 registered no corresponding increase, a pattern consistent with the effect of government support measures. From 2021 insolvencies rose to the highest levels in the series, reaching approximately 24,500 in 2024.

2026 (to August) shown in grey as a partial year. The insolvent-only series validates to within a few per cent of the Insolvency Service's published totals.

The London Gazette · composition of exit

Not every winding-up is a failure.

The insolvent share of dated exits fell from roughly 89 per cent in 2006 to 48 per cent in 2020, before rising again to 67 per cent in 2024. The mid-period decline reflects a tax-driven surge in solvent members' voluntary liquidations, whereby owners closed healthy companies to realise gains; the recent rise reflects the post-pandemic insolvency wave.

Solvent and insolvent winding-up notices are captured differently in the Gazette, so read the split as indicative of the trend rather than exact.

Companies House & the London Gazette

Insolvency risk is greatest for firms of intermediate size.

Failure rates are hump-shaped in firm size. Small (5.3 per cent) and medium (5.1 per cent) firms fail at a higher rate than micro (3.1 per cent) or large firms (3.0 per cent). The elevated rate observed among firms lacking usable accounts is treated as a distress signal rather than a size category and is excluded.

Companies House & the London Gazette

Failure concentrates in hospitality.

Licensed restaurants (9.2 per cent) and public houses (7.7 per cent) exhibit insolvency rates approximately three times those of road freight or wholesale, with construction trades occupying an intermediate position. The pattern accords with the thin margins and demand sensitivity characteristic of these sectors.

Companies House & the London Gazette

The year of formation shapes survival.

Among firms grouped by year of incorporation, the proportion becoming insolvent within five years is highest for cohorts entering shortly before the 2008 crisis and lowest for those of the calmer mid-2010s, before rising again for the 2018–19 cohorts. The macroeconomic environment into which a firm is born materially affects its early survival.

Companies House · Gross Value Added

Gross Value Added (staff costs plus operating profit plus depreciation) requires a full profit-and-loss statement, which most companies are exempt from filing. It is computable for only 0.17 per cent of the panel. Where computable it totals approximately £297 billion across those firm-years, a median of £3.2 million per firm and about £50,000 per employee. Economy-wide GVA is properly measured by the ONS Annual Business Survey.

Part III · Listed firms and the 2008 crisis
FTSE 100 · balanced panel

UK large-cap value has not recovered from 2008.

A balanced panel of FTSE 100 constituents with continuous market-capitalisation coverage permits an unbiased measure of the crisis and its aftermath. Indexed to 2007, the panel's aggregate value fell by 44 per cent in 2008 and, seventeen years later, remained approximately 6 per cent below its pre-crisis level. As the panel comprises only firms that survived and remained listed, it excludes the period's principal casualties and therefore constitutes a conservative estimate of the destruction of value.

FTSE 100 · dividends through the crisis

UK large-cap dividends halved in 2009 and never recovered.

The same balanced panel of continuously-listed constituents paid out in dividends in 2007. As the crisis forced banks and others to suspend distributions, the panel's total dividends fell by roughly half in 2009 and, indexed to 2007, remained about a third below the pre-crisis level in 2024 — with a second, sharp cut in the pandemic year of 2020.

FTSE 100

The crisis reshuffled the index.

Index turnover corroborates the disruption: eighteen to nineteen constituents entered or left the FTSE 100 in 2007 and 2008, against seven in the calmer years of 2010 to 2012.

S&P 500

The deeper US index churns harder.

The S&P 500 replaced thirty-five to thirty-eight constituents annually at the height of the crisis, a markedly higher rate of turnover than the FTSE 100, reflecting both its greater breadth and the intensity of reallocation within the US market.

S&P 500 · earnings through the crisis

American earnings collapsed, then nearly doubled.

The median S&P 500 constituent earned $575m in 2007. Profits more than halved through the crisis, to $238m in 2009, before recovering and continuing to grow: by 2024 the median constituent earned $968m, some two-thirds above its pre-crisis level. Where UK large-cap distributions stagnated, US large-cap earnings rebounded and expanded.

S&P 500 · industry composition

US value is concentrated in technology; the FTSE's is not.

In 2023 information technology accounted for approximately 31 per cent of S&P 500 market value but only 1 per cent of the FTSE 100. The two indices differ in structure across the board: American value concentrates in technology, whereas the FTSE's largest sectors are financials, consumer staples and industrials. IT's share of S&P value has roughly doubled since 2010; a continuous series is omitted because the annual market-capitalisation data is too sparse in the most recent years to support a reliable trend.

FTSE 100 · index structure since 1984

The FTSE never grew a technology sector.

Every constituent the index has held since 1984 has been classified by hand (the statutory codes being holding-company dominated). The result reveals a persistent structural feature: information technology has accounted for approximately 2 per cent of FTSE 100 membership throughout, without trend. Over the same period consumer staples contracted from roughly 20 to 10 per cent and financials expanded from 15 to 23 per cent. The composition rotated among mature sectors while the scale-technology segment that came to dominate the S&P 500 never emerged.

FTSE 100 × S&P 500

The valuation gap.

Framed after Becker, Benmelech and Monteiro (2026), which documents a United States–Europe equity valuation gap concentrated in technology-intensive sectors. In 2023 the median S&P 500 constituent traded at 1.11 times the book value of its assets, against 0.76 times for the FTSE 100. Information technology accounts for approximately 31 per cent of S&P 500 market value and approximately 1 per cent of the FTSE 100 by market value, indicating that the valuation gap is substantially a gap in technology concentration.

Confined to the leading indices rather than the full listed universe; UK sector shares use an analyst hand-classification of every constituent, the statutory codes being holding-company dominated. Market-to-book is a Tobin's-Q-style proxy (market value / total assets); the gap survives excluding financials (S&P 1.29x vs FTSE 0.90x) and the FTSE figure rests on the ~75 constituents with total-assets data (fundamentals from 2022).

S&P 500 & FTSE 100 · concentration

US value is concentrating in a handful of names.

The ten largest S&P 500 constituents accounted for about 21 per cent of the index's value in 2015 and 34 per cent by 2023 — the rise of the “Magnificent Seven.” The FTSE 100 has long been concentrated, its top ten steady at around 43 per cent; the American market is converging on that level rather than remaining broad.

Forthcoming · declining business dynamism

Is British business dynamism declining?

The central question in the business-dynamism literature — whether entry rates are falling and the firm population ageing, as documented for the United States — is answerable from this database, but not yet reliably. Both measures depend on knowing which companies are still trading, and the death record is currently incomplete: dissolved firms are not always marked as such, and the earliest years under-observe long-lived firms.

A dedicated extraction is closing that gap now. A background process is querying the Companies House API for every dead or former company — recovering incorporation date, dissolution date, status and industry — so that the active population can be measured precisely in every year. Once complete, the entry rate and the age structure of the corporate population can be estimated on a consistent basis, and the question answered directly.
