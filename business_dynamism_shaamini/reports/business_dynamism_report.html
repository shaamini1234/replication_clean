3. Insolvencies (Gazette)

Two kinds of "death date" — they're different events, not duplicates:
  • Gazette insolvency date (this section) = economic death — when and why a firm entered a formal insolvency procedure (liquidation, administration, court order). The best death date for dynamism.
  • Companies House dissolution date (from the API extraction, in ch_company_profile) = legal death — when the firm was struck off the register, usually 1–3 years after the insolvency onset. Crucially, it's the only death signal for strike-offs — firms that closed without insolvency and never appear in the Gazette.
Rule of thumb: anchor on the Gazette onset where it exists; fall back to the CH dissolution date for strike-off firms.

4b. Gross Value Added (GVA) — computable coverage

GVA ≈ staff costs + operating profit + depreciation. All three are columns in the accounts, but all three are behind the "P&L wall", so GVA only exists where a firm filed full accounts.

Input parameterCoverage of all filings
staff costs~1%
operating profit~1%
depreciation~1–2%
all three present → GVA computable60,440 firm-years (0.15%)

So GVA is a large/medium-firm metric, not a population one — for economy-wide GVA use ONS ABS aGVA.

Computable-GVA firm-years, by size of firm

Skewed to medium & large firms — they're the ones that file a full P&L. Micro firms almost never appear.

Productivity by size — a deliberate non-result

GVA per employee in this covered subset is roughly £40–70k — the right UK ballpark. But we do not compare productivity across size bands from this data: which firms file a full P&L differs systematically by size, so any size ranking would reflect who chose to file, not real productivity. For productivity-by-size, use ONS ABS approximate-GVA — a properly grossed, representative sample. This subset is a selected large/medium-firm metric, useful for firm-level GVA levels, not for size comparisons.

Companies by how they join across sources

"No insolvency recorded" = trading or struck off (strike-offs aren't in the Gazette), so it is not the same as "still alive". Log scale.

Birth dates: sourced vs estimated

How the estimate works: UK company numbers are issued in sequence, so a number pins roughly when a firm was born. We learn the number→year mapping from the 5.7M live companies (which have both number and real date), then read off the year for dead firms with no surviving record. Validated on 2.5M held-out companies: correct to the year. Year-only, flagged birth_is_estimated=true, no source URL (it's derived, not looked up) — so it's easy to exclude from evidence-grade work.

Part B — What it reveals (analysis)

1. The "valley of death" — age at insolvency

Insolvency risk peaks at ages 2–4 and tapers with age; the median failing firm is 6–7 years old (stable across the 2000s, 2010s and 2020s). Young firms are the fragile ones — survive the early years and the hazard drops.

2. Which industries fail most

Hospitality leads by far — licensed restaurants 9.2% and pubs 7.7% insolvent, roughly 3× the rate of freight or wholesale (~3%). Construction trades cluster in between. Thin margins and demand-sensitivity show up exactly where you'd expect.

3. Insolvency rate by firm size

Failure is hump-shaped: small (5.3%) and medium (5.1%) firms fail more than micro (3.1%) or large (3.0%). Micro firms often sit dormant rather than fail; the largest have buffers; the squeezed middle is most exposed.

Firms with no usable size/accounts data have a much higher observed rate (~13%) — missing accounts is itself a distress signal, not a real "size band", so it's excluded from the chart.

4. Cohort effect — birth year vs early failure

Of firms born in each year, the share insolvent within 5 years: highest for the 2008 crisis cohort (3.9%), lowest for the calm mid-2010s (1.8%), and rising again for 2018–19 cohorts — when you're born into the economy shapes your survival odds.

5. Geography — where firms are, and where they fail

Insolvencies concentrate where companies are — London (137k), the North West and South East lead on count. Registered-office postcode was recoverable for 81% of insolvencies.

Coverage caveat: Scotland (8k) and Northern Ireland (5k) are heavily under-counted — their insolvencies mostly use separate procedures/notices outside our 8 codes. This data is England & Wales-complete but under-covers Scotland/NI. Region derived from postcode area (approximate; precise region needs the ONS Postcode Directory). A true insolvency rate by region needs contemporaneous company stock, so counts are shown, not rates.

6. US vs UK large-caps — value-gap context

Framing after Becker, Benmelech & Monteiro (NBER 2026), "The Value Gap: Europe Cannot Scale": US listed value is concentrated in high-growth tech; Europe's isn't. Our S&P 500 vs FTSE 100 tables show the same, for the top indices.

Valuation: market value ÷ book assets (2023)

US large-caps trade at ~1.0× book assets vs the UK's 0.81× — a ~24% valuation premium, visible even top-index to top-index. (A Tobin's-Q-style ratio, not the paper's exact Q, but the same "value gap" directionally.)

Firm size: median total assets (2023)

The median S&P 500 firm holds ~$28bn in assets vs ~$15bn (£11.8bn) for the median FTSE 100 firm — US large-caps are roughly 2× larger, mirroring the paper's rightward-shifted US size distribution.

Top-index only (S&P ~478 vs FTSE ~52 GBP-reporters), not the paper's all-listed universe; UK figures converted at ~£1=$1.27. Directional, not a full replication.

Technology's share of index market value

US large-cap value is ~32% technology; UK large-cap value is only ~6%. The value gap the paper documents is, in large part, a technology-concentration gap — the UK's top index is holding companies, pharma, banks and miners, not scale-tech.

UK sector split is omitted as a clean chart: most FTSE firms file a holding-company SIC ("head office activities" ≈ 57%), so UK SIC is an unreliable sector proxy — the 6% tech figure is a rough lower bound.
