UK company insolvencies — a first look

Gazette insolvency notices joined to Companies House accounts · every UK company, 2008–2026

This dataset links two public records: which companies became insolvent (official notices in The Gazette) and their finances (accounts filed at Companies House). Below are the first things it tells us, in plain terms.

What "insolvency" means here. Insolvency is one specific way a company exits: it can't pay its debts and enters a formal procedure — creditors' liquidation, a court winding-up order, administration, or receivership. It is not the only way companies close (most are quietly "struck off" the register, a separate non-insolvency route not covered here). Insolvency is the economically important, precisely-dated, publicly-recorded kind of failure. We also flag solvent wind-ups (healthy companies choosing to close) so they aren't mistaken for failures.

1. How many companies become insolvent each year

Each bar is the number of companies entering an insolvent procedure that year (liquidations, court wind-ups, administrations), from 2002 to today. The record runs back to 1998 (earlier years are still loading). Notice the shape — it isn't random.

Why this matters: the peaks match real history — low in the mid-2000s boom, spiking at the 2008 financial crisis, a calm mid-2010s, then a sharp rise from 2022 (cost-of-living pressure, end of COVID support). When a dataset you built reproduces known events, it's telling the truth.

2. What insolvent companies look like financially

Using each firm's last accounts before the insolvency (and the latest accounts for firms still trading), here is the typical — median — company in each group:

"Net worth" = what a company owns minus what it owes. "Underwater" = it owes more than it owns (negative net worth) — a warning sign of distress.

Insolvent firms are small and financially exhausted: a typical one has ~£12k of assets, almost no net worth, and 38% were already underwater before the formal insolvency. Solvent wind-ups are the mirror image — cash-rich, debt-free companies closing by choice (£152k net worth, only 4% underwater). The two are genuinely different events, and the numbers prove it.

Insolvency is overwhelmingly a small-business story: 94% of insolvent firms are micro or small; large companies are just 0.2%.

A typical failing firm has a net worth around £3,250 five years out, and it drains steadily — £2,200, £1,240, £500 — down to almost nothing (£24) in its final year. Insolvency is rarely sudden; the accounts show it coming.

4. Insolvent firms go quiet first

On average a company files its last accounts about 16 months before its insolvency notice. The silence itself is an early warning — firms stop filing well before they formally fail.

5. Most companies are trading — and that's the point

Of the 7.9M companies, 93% have no insolvency on record (still trading, or closed another way). Only ~4% are insolvencies with matching accounts, ~3.5% became insolvent without ever filing (tiny or dormant firms). Keeping the trading companies in view is what lets us measure insolvency rates, not just counts.

Honest limits (worth knowing): this covers insolvency only — companies quietly struck off the register are a separate, larger route of closure not included here. Revenue and profit are missing for most small firms by law, so the financial picture leans on the balance sheet. And the "insolvency date" is when the procedure began, which is the right economic moment — often years before a company is formally dissolved.
